Reading a Solar Datasheet Without Lying to Yourself

// STC is the marketing copy

A 400 W panel at STC delivers 400 W only at 1000 W/m², 25 °C cell temperature, and air mass 1.5. Your roof at noon in Bandung will give you maybe 80% of that. This is a walk through what each number on a solar datasheet actually means, and a way to estimate real yield without trusting anyone.

Cover shot— 10 W mono panel + electronic load + DMMs on the bench. The setup I used for §05.

01 / What STC is and isn't

Standard Test Conditions is a lab benchmark: 1000 W/m² irradiance, 25 °C cell temperature, AM 1.5 spectrum. Every manufacturer rates panels here, because under STC the same panel always reads the same. It's reproducible. It's also fictional in most operating conditions, in three ways:

  • 1000 W/m² is peak clear-sky irradiance. Bandung at noon under clear sky maxes around 950 W/m². On a cloudy afternoon: 200–400.
  • 25 °C cell temperature is what cells reach only in a chamber. Real panels on a tropical roof at noon sit at 50–65 °C.
  • AM 1.5 is a continental spectrum. ITB Bandung at the equator gets closer to AM 1.0–1.2 at solar noon — slightly different spectrum content.

So the rated power is a ceiling, not a midpoint. NOCT (Nominal Operating Cell Temperature) on the same datasheet, usually 45 °C, is a more realistic anchor point.

02 / The temperature derate, in numbers

Every solar cell datasheet lists three temperature coefficients:

  • α_Isc — short-circuit current, typically +0.05 %/°C (slight benefit from warmth)
  • β_Voc — open-circuit voltage, typically −0.30 %/°C (significant loss)
  • γ_Pmax — max power, typically −0.35 %/°C (the one that matters)

The cell temperature at any given moment is approximately:

T_cell ≈ T_ambient + (NOCT − 20) · (G / 800)

Where G is irradiance in W/m². For Bandung at solar noon: T_amb ≈ 28 °C, G ≈ 900 W/m², NOCT = 45 °C:

T_cell ≈ 28 + (45-20) · (900/800) = 28 + 28 = 56 °C

Now apply the derate:

P_actual ≈ P_STC · (G/1000) · [1 + γ_Pmax · (T_cell − 25)]
        = 400 · 0.9 · [1 + (-0.0035) · (56-25)]
        = 400 · 0.9 · 0.892
        ≈ 321 W

That's 80% of nameplate. Typical for a tropical install. If you've built your system budget on STC numbers, you're already 20% over.

03 / The I-V curve is the real spec sheet

Numbers in a table hide structure. The I-V curve shows it all: Isc on the left, Voc on the right, the knee between them where Pmax lives. The shape of the knee is the fill factor:

FF = (Vmp · Imp) / (Voc · Isc)

Good monocrystalline cells: FF ≥ 0.78. Polycrystalline: 0.72–0.75. Anything below 0.7 — the panel is degraded, mismatched, or you measured wrong.

What the curve also reveals: bypass diode activation under partial shading. Cover one cell with your hand on a real panel and the I-V curve develops a step. A well-designed system uses bypass diodes (and string-level optimizers, if you can afford them) to keep that step from collapsing the entire string.

04 / Try this yourself — three test paths

1. PVsyst free trial — the industry-standard yield-estimation tool. Model your roof tilt, azimuth, shading, and panel datasheet. It runs an annual hour-by-hour simulation using TMY weather files. For Bandung, use the SOLARGIS or NASA POWER dataset.

2. pvlib-python (open source). If you want to actually understand what PVsyst is doing under the hood, pvlib is the same physics in 200 lines of Python. The five-parameter De Soto model lets you take a datasheet and produce a full I-V curve at any temperature and irradiance.

import pvlib
params = pvlib.pvsystem.calcparams_desoto(
    effective_irradiance=900,
    temp_cell=56,
    alpha_sc=0.05e-2 * I_sc_stc,
    a_ref=1.6,
    I_L_ref=I_sc_stc,
    I_o_ref=1e-9,
    R_sh_ref=400,
    R_s=0.4,
)
curve = pvlib.pvsystem.singlediode(*params)
print(curve['p_mp'])  # actual Pmax at these conditions

3. LTspice with a PV macromodel. Texas Instruments and others publish Spice models for solar cells (single-diode + parasitic resistors). Apply different temperatures and irradiance current sources, sweep load resistance, plot V-I and P-V. Great for understanding MPPT design before you commit to a charge controller.

Fig. 02— Measured Pmax vs. cell temperature, 5 trials on a 10 W panel. Slope matches manufacturer's γ_Pmax within 10%.

05 / A real-bench test for $20

Buy a small 10 W mono panel. Hook it through an ammeter to a variable load — a rheostat, or a stack of resistors with a switch, or a small electronic load. Take it outside in two conditions:

  1. Mid-morning, sun behind you, panel cool: trace I-V from short-circuit to open-circuit.
  2. Solar noon, panel hot to the touch (or with an IR thermometer pointed at the back): trace it again.

Plot both. The afternoon curve will shift down and to the left — Voc visibly lower, Isc slightly higher, Pmax meaningfully reduced. This is the moment STC stops feeling abstract.

06 / What I still don't know

I haven't internalized how much the spectrum difference between AM 1.5 and equatorial AM 1.2 actually buys or costs us. The literature suggests it's small (~2%) and direction-dependent (favours wider-bandgap cells), but I'd want to measure it. If you've got data, point me at it.
