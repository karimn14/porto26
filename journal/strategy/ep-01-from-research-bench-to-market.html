Research Bench to Market — Six Books, Five Takeaways

// A reading roundup for hardware-heavy founders

I've been reading anything I can find on deeptech founder paths for a year. Six books, three industry reports, half a dozen long-form essays. Here are the five ideas that survived being tested against the others — the ones I think I'll still believe in five years.

The pile, roughly— six books, a stack of printed reports, a notebook of margin notes.

01 / Why this reading project

The thing nobody tells you about Physics Engineering at ITB is that the bridge from "I built a thing that works in the lab" to "this is now a product someone pays for" is mostly invisible from inside the lab. The professors who taught me MPC and circuit design were not, in general, people who had crossed that bridge. Every paper ends at "future work."

So I went looking. Not for inspiration — I went looking for maps. People who had crossed and were willing to draw the route afterwards. What follows are the five takeaways that kept appearing across the sources, in different vocabularies, and that I have started using as a frame for my own decisions about what to build next.

02 / Takeaway 1 — Tech-push and market-pull are not opposites, they're a sequence

Christensen (Innovator's Dilemma) frames disruption as market-pull: customers underserved by incumbents pull in cheaper alternatives. Pisano (Creative Construction) frames hardware innovation as tech-push: deep R&D produces capabilities that then find markets. The deeptech community treats these as a binary. Reading them together makes the truth obvious: it's a sequence.

Hardware founders almost always start tech-push: someone in a lab figures out how to do a thing nobody else can. The first eighteen months are about understanding the technology's actual envelope — what it can and cannot do, at what cost. Only after that does market-pull become useful, because only then do you know what customer needs the tech can credibly serve.

The mistake I see in undergrad pitch decks is jumping to market-pull while still uncertain about tech-push. "Our drone disinfects warehouses" sounds market-pull, but if you haven't proved your spray nozzle works at 95% coverage in repeated flights, you haven't earned the customer story yet.

03 / Takeaway 2 — Hardware loves patient capital. Mismatched capital kills more good ideas than bad engineering does

Atomico's 2023 Deeptech report and a thread of Lux Capital essays make the same point: software's three-year venture cycle does not match hardware's seven-year capability cycle. A hardware company that takes VC money structured like a SaaS company will burn through it before reaching TRL 7, then get downround-killed at the worst possible moment — usually right before manufacturing readiness.

The interventions that work are:

  • Grant capital first. Government deeptech grants (in Indonesia: BRIN, LPDP innovation tracks) buy 18–36 months of bench-to-prototype time without dilution.
  • Corporate strategic capital. A large customer that pays for development in advance is, effectively, patient capital with a built-in first customer.
  • VC only after TRL 6. Cleantech-style deeptech funds (Breakthrough Energy, EQT Ventures, regional equivalents) underwrite longer cycles.

04 / Takeaway 3 — Effectuation beats causation for the first eighteen months

Sarasvathy's research on expert entrepreneurs (Effectuation, 2008) is the single most useful framework I have read for the lab-to-market transition. The short version: experienced founders don't pick a goal and work backward to resources. They look at the resources they already have — including skills, network, and the specific tech they built — and explore what affordable next-step actions those resources enable.

For an undergraduate Physics Engineer in Bandung this is liberating. I don't need to identify the global market for autonomous drones and reverse-engineer the path to it. I need to look at: my thesis, my access to the URO lab, the three companies in Bandung that already pay interns to work on autonomous systems, and the friends-of-friends who run small agtech operations. What affordable next-step does that combination enable? Effectuation says: do that next-step, see what new options open, iterate.

This sounds obvious. It is not how most engineering students approach career thinking, including me until I read it.

05 / Takeaway 4 — The valley of death is real but mappable

"Valley of death" is the gap between TRL 3 (proven concept) and TRL 7 (system demonstrated in operational environment). The valley exists because TRL 4–6 is where you spend the most money for the least obvious progress. The prototype works, the demo video looks great, but you still don't have a manufactured product. Investors who funded the demo expect a manufactured product. The gap is funded by neither grant capital (which usually runs out at TRL 3) nor commercial VC (which usually wants TRL 7 first).

NASA's TRL doc and the EU's H2020 guide both publish typical funding profiles. McKinsey's 2022 deeptech piece adds: the median deeptech company spends 60% of its total pre-revenue capital in the TRL 4–6 valley. Knowing that in advance changes how you plan rounds.

Mapping the valley for your own project: write out each rung. Estimate cost per rung. Identify, for each rung, which capital source is appropriate. Most students never do this exercise. The ones who do raise smarter.

06 / Takeaway 5 — Indonesian deeptech: government is the most underrated customer

This one is regional. In the US and EU, deeptech founders sell to private enterprise first. In Indonesia the structure is different: the most reliable big-ticket buyer of deeptech is the state — through BUMN procurement, defense, agriculture (Kementerian Pertanian), or the energy ministry. Three Indonesian deeptech founders I've heard speak — at ITB Innovation Day and at Pustena seminars — all said the same thing: get one government anchor customer signed before raising private money. The presence of that anchor reduces every downstream conversation by an order of magnitude.

This is unromantic. It is not the Y Combinator story. It is also, by every account I have heard from people who actually built deeptech in Indonesia, true.

07 / The reading list

In rough order of usefulness for an undergraduate planning a deeptech career:

  • Sarasvathy — Effectuation: Elements of Entrepreneurial Expertise (2008). The single book I'd recommend if you only read one.
  • Pisano — Creative Construction (2019). Argues that big-company innovation and small-company innovation require different recipes.
  • Christensen — The Innovator's Dilemma (1997). Classic. Read for the framework, skim the case studies.
  • Reis — The Lean Startup (2011). Software-flavored, but the build-measure-learn loop translates to hardware iteration.
  • Atomico — State of European Deeptech 2023 (free PDF). The data on funding cycles and time-to-revenue is the part I keep coming back to.
  • McKinsey — The Next Big Arenas of Competition (2023). Less technical, more strategic. Useful for mapping where deeptech capital is flowing globally.

I also read a stack of essays from Bret Victor, Patrick Collison, and the Atomico deeptech newsletter. None of those are books, but they're worth the time.

08 / What's on the next shelf

Three books I'm queuing up next, partly because the previous six kept pointing at them: Hwang's Rainforest (regional innovation ecosystems), Ries' The Startup Way (corporate intrapreneurship), and a manufacturing book — probably Jeffrey Liker's The Toyota Way — once I'm closer to needing to think about MRL.
