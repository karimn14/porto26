Crystal Load Capacitance, Calculated

// The Math I Used to Skip

I populated a brand-new STM32 board with 22 pF caps on the crystal — same as I always do — and it ran 5,000 ppm off frequency. The problem was one number I had been guessing for two years.

Bench shot— the offending board, with the 22 pF caps that should have been 27 pF, soldered next to an 8 MHz crystal.

01 / The crystal that nearly worked

I'd been routing the same crystal circuit since my first STM32 build. 8 MHz crystal, two 22 pF caps from each pin to ground, a 1 MΩ feedback resistor. Done. It worked every time — until this board, where the timing was so far off that USB enumeration failed.

The crystal was a generic 8 MHz, but I hadn't bothered to read its load capacitance spec. That number, CL, is the whole game. Every crystal datasheet gives you one. It tells you what total capacitance the oscillator network must present to the crystal for it to run at its rated frequency.

02 / What CL actually means

The Pierce oscillator topology (used by basically every modern MCU) puts the crystal between two pins, with shunt caps C1 and C2 from each pin to ground. The crystal sees these in series, plus the stray capacitance of the traces and the MCU pins:

CL = (C1 · C2) / (C1 + C2)  +  C_stray

For the symmetric case C1 = C2 (most common):

CL = C1 / 2  +  C_stray

Solving for the cap value:

C1 = 2 · (CL − C_stray)

The crystal manufacturer wants to see, say, 18 pF. If C_stray is around 5 pF on your layout, you need C1 = 2(18−5) = 26 pF. The nearest E12 value is 27 pF. Use 22 pF and you'll be feeding the crystal 16 pF instead of 18 — and the oscillation frequency will pull high by tens to hundreds of ppm.

Fig. 01— LTspice FFT of the Pierce oscillator output, swept across CL values. Peak shifts ~80 ppm per 5 pF of CL.

02b / Where the stray comes from

The C_stray term is what gets people. It is real, it is non-zero, and there's no datasheet for it. Three contributions matter:

  • MCU pin capacitance. Each OSC pin has 2–4 pF to ground internally. STM32 datasheets list this as C_io in the pin characteristics.
  • PCB trace and pad capacitance. Each short trace from MCU to crystal pad to ground cap is 0.5–2 pF.
  • Crystal package capacitance. Usually folded into the manufacturer's spec, but check the datasheet's C0 (motional vs. shunt) note.

Round number: assume 4–7 pF total C_stray for a typical small layout. The closer you place the caps to the crystal, the lower it goes.

03 / How wrong does wrong feel?

The frequency pulling formula for a parallel-resonant crystal is:

Δf / f ≈ − C_motional / [ 2 · (C0 + CL)² ] · δCL

For a typical 8 MHz crystal (C_motional ≈ 12 fF, C0 ≈ 3 pF), being 5 pF off on CL shifts the frequency by roughly 40–60 ppm. Two pF off is 15–25 ppm. UART will usually still work at 100 ppm error. USB will not — the spec requires <500 ppm, and full-speed enumeration cliffs hard when you're past that.

Worse: too high a load capacitance and the loop gain margin can drop below 1 — the oscillator won't start, or starts unreliably with temperature. The "internal RC backup" you didn't think you had quietly takes over and your timing is suddenly nothing like 8 MHz.

Fig. 02— Measured frequency vs. shunt cap pair, three trials. Linear fit slope agrees with the manufacturer's pulling curve within 18%.

04 / Try this yourself — Pierce in LTspice

Pierce oscillators are quick to simulate. The crystal equivalent circuit is a series R-L-C (motional arm) in parallel with a shunt capacitance C0:

* 8 MHz crystal: typical parameters
.param Lm=0.0398 Cm=10f Rm=8 C0=3p
* CL sweep through C1=C2
.param Cl 22p
* Pierce
M1 out in 0 0 CMOS_inv
Rb in out 1Meg
Xq in xtal Cm Lm Rm
Cshunt in xtal {C0}
C1 in 0 {Cl}
C2 xtal 0 {Cl}
.step param Cl list 12p 18p 22p 27p 33p
.tran 0 200u 100u 1n

Sweep Cl and FFT the output. You'll see the oscillation peak shift hundreds of ppm across the sweep. Add 5 pF of stray (an extra cap from in to ground) to see how the math from §02 plays out empirically.

05 / A real-bench test for ~$5

Order one crystal (CL=18pF, 8MHz, sub-$1 from LCSC) and three sets of caps: 18 pF, 22 pF, 33 pF. With a Blue Pill (STM32F103, ~$2) and a frequency counter or a scope with FFT:

  1. Configure MCO output to HSE/1 (8 MHz on PA8). Measure with the counter.
  2. Swap the cap pair for each set. Let the board warm up 60 s between measurements.
  3. Plot frequency vs. cap value. The slope per pF will match the math within 20%.

This is one of those experiments where the math feels academic until you see your own scope agree with it. Then it sticks.

06 / The pre-flight checklist

  • Find CL on the crystal datasheet. Write it down.
  • Estimate C_stray (5 pF default; 4 pF if you're tight; 7 pF if loose).
  • Solve C1 = 2(CL − C_stray). Pick the nearest E12 value.
  • Place caps directly beside the crystal pads. Traces under 5 mm. No vias in the loop.
  • Pour ground around but never through the oscillator loop.
  • Document the chosen C1/C2 values in the schematic with a note. Future-you will thank you.
