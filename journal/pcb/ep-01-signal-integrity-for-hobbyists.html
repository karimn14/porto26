Return Paths and the Lies of the Ground Plane

// Signal Integrity for Hobbyists

I broke my first hobby board with a clock line — not the trace itself, but the return current path that ran under it. This is the post I wish someone had handed me before I cut my first ground plane.

Hand-drawn map of the offending board. Slot in the ground plane, directly under the clock line.Hand-drawn map of the offending board. Slot in the ground plane, directly under the clock line.

01 / The trace was fine. The return wasn't.

The board was a simple ESP32 audio recorder. One 24 MHz clock line ran from the codec to the MCU, about 18 mm long, neatly routed. The board worked — for about ten minutes at a time. Then it would randomly reset, lose I²S sync, or boot into a corrupted state.

I spent two days hunting it. Power rail noise looked fine on the scope. Decoupling caps were where they should be. I'd done everything the YouTube videos said. Then I noticed: I had carved a thin slot in the ground plane to route a separate analog supply for the microphone — directly under the clock trace.

That was it. The slot wasn't even cutting the signal — it was forcing the return current to take a long detour. The math of why this matters is one of the most important things I've learned about layout.

Fig. 01— Return current density under a clock trace, simulated with FastHenry. Concentration directly under the trace, except where the slot forces a detour.

02 / Where current actually returns

Every signal has a partner: the return current. Out the driver, through the trace, into the load, back through ground. At DC, the return spreads through the ground plane along the path of least resistance. At higher frequency — even 1 MHz, let alone 24 — it concentrates along the path of least inductance. That path is directly under the trace.

Howard Johnson made this point in High-Speed Digital Design and Eric Bogatin has been re-explaining it patiently on YouTube for fifteen years. The transition happens roughly when:

f >> 1 / (2π · L_loop · R_plane)

For typical 2-layer FR4 boards, that's tens of kHz. So anything digital you're doing is already in the return-under-trace regime.

When you cut a slot, slot it, or even just thin the ground plane between the source and the load, you force that return current to take a longer loop. Larger loop = larger inductance = larger V = L·(di/dt) noise spike injected into the rail. EMI escapes. Adjacent traces couple. Ground bounce ruins your reference.

03 / The math, conservatively

For a microstrip with width W over a plane at height H, loop inductance per unit length is roughly:

L' ≈ μ₀ · (H / W)        [for W > H]

For a 0.25 mm trace at 0.4 mm above the plane (a typical hobbyist 2-layer stackup), L' ≈ 0.6 nH/mm. A clean 18 mm clock trace has loop inductance around 11 nH.

Now slot the plane. The return current has to detour around the slot — call it a 10 mm extra path with the return separated by 1 mm from the signal. The detour adds roughly 5 nH/mm of extra series inductance just on the return leg. Same 18 mm trace now has 50–60 nH of loop inductance.

For a 3.3 V CMOS clock with 1 ns edges and an estimated I≈30 mA edge current:

V_spike = L · di/dt = 60nH · 30mA/1ns ≈ 1.8 V

That's a 1.8 V noise spike superimposed on your nominal 3.3 V rail, twice per clock period. No wonder things reset.

Fig. 02— Eye diagrams from LTspice for three different return-path inductances. 0 nH (clean), 5 nH (degraded), 50 nH (closed).

04 / Try this yourself — in simulation

You don't need a board to see this. Three ways to test it without fabrication:

1. LTspice or ngspice — return-path model. Model the signal line as a transmission line (TLINE primitive), and add a series inductor on the return path representing the detour. Sweep the inductor value from 0 nH to 50 nH and plot the eye diagram at the receiver. The closing eye tells the story.

* Microstrip with controllable return
V1 in 0 PULSE(0 3.3 0 1n 1n 20n 40n)
T1 in gndA out gndB ZO=50 TD=120p
Lret gndB 0 {Lret}        ; the detour inductance
.step param Lret list 0n 5n 20n 50n
.tran 0.01n 200n

2. KiCad + ngspice (or QUCS Studio). Draw two test boards: one with a solid plane, one with a 1 mm slot. Use a free 2-D field solver — FastHenry, or the inductance extractor in QUCS — to compute loop inductance. The numbers will match the back-of-envelope above within a factor of two.

3. Proteus VSM. If you have access, drop a 74HC04 chain, route a square wave, and compare a board with a continuous plane to one with a series inductor between source and load grounds. Look at the receiver waveform and at the radiated emission monitor (Proteus has one).

05 / What I'm doing differently now

Three rules, learned the hard way:

  • Never cut a return path under a switching trace. If you must split the ground plane (mixed-signal designs are a real reason), keep all high-speed signals on one side of the cut.
  • Stitch with vias generously. Where digital and analog ground need to be one star-point, use a single fat trace or a deliberate ferrite, not an accidental crack.
  • Watch the return on every clock and every CS line. They're the worst offenders.

I still don't have intuition for sub-MHz signals. If you've got a hobbyist-budget way to characterize that crossover empirically, I'd love to hear about it.
